01 / THE BASICS

What is Data Analysis?

Data analysis is the process of examining information to find useful answers, patterns and insights.

Data is information—like marks, prices, attendance or sales. Analysis means studying that information carefully.

We organize and compare data to understand what is happening and help decide what to do next.

Everyday example: You compare phone prices, battery life and reviews before choosing a phone. You're using information to make a decision!

Important Notes Every Data Analysis Student Should Write

Keep these basic concepts in your notebook. They are useful for revision, practice and interviews.

1. Key Definitions
  • Data: Raw facts, figures or information collected for a purpose.
  • Data Analysis: Examining data to answer questions, identify patterns and make better decisions.
  • Dataset: A collection of related data, often arranged in rows and columns.
  • Data Analyst: A person who studies data to help people or organizations make informed decisions.
  • Insight: A useful finding discovered through analysis.
  • Dashboard: A visual display of key information and measures.
  • Report: A structured presentation of findings and results.
2. Types of Data

Numerical data: Values expressed as numbers.

  • Discrete: Countable values (number of students, items sold).
  • Continuous: Measured values (height, weight, temperature).

Categorical data: Values describing categories or groups (city, product, department, status).

3. Understand a Dataset
  • Row: A record or observation (e.g., one sale or one student).
  • Column: A field or variable (e.g., date, product, quantity, price).
  • Cell: The value where a row and column intersect.
  • Header: The column name describing the field.
  • CSV: A common text file format for tabular data.

5. Data Cleaning

Data cleaning helps improve the quality and reliability of a dataset.

  • Check missing or blank values.
  • Remove or investigate duplicate records.
  • Correct spelling and inconsistent categories.
  • Check incorrect values and data types.
  • Check date, currency and number formats.
  • Investigate unusual values (outliers)—do not remove them automatically.
6. Common Calculations
  • Sum / Total: Adds values together.
  • Mean: Sum of values ÷ number of values.
  • Median: Middle value after sorting.
  • Mode: Most frequently occurring value.
  • Percentage: (Part ÷ Total) × 100.
  • Range: Maximum value − Minimum value.
Remember: The mean can be affected by extreme values. The median may better represent the centre of some datasets.
7. Choosing a Chart
  • Bar chart: Compare categories.
  • Line chart: Show change over time.
  • Pie chart: Show simple parts of a whole (use sparingly).
  • Histogram: Show distribution of numerical values.
  • Scatter plot: Explore relationships between two numerical variables.
8. Important Business Terms
  • Revenue: Money earned from sales before deducting costs.
  • Profit: Revenue minus costs and expenses (as defined for the calculation).
  • KPI: Key Performance Indicator—a measure used to track a goal.
  • Trend: A general pattern of change over time.
  • Correlation: A relationship between variables; correlation alone does not prove causation.
  • Outlier: A value unusually far from others in a dataset.
9. Responsible Data Use
  • Protect personal and confidential information.
  • Use data only for an appropriate purpose.
  • Check the source and reliability of data.
  • Avoid misleading charts or conclusions.
  • Explain limitations and uncertainty.
  • Do not confuse correlation with causation.
10. Questions to Ask When You Receive a Dataset
  1. What does each row represent?
  2. What do the columns mean?
  3. Where did the data come from?
  4. What question am I trying to answer?
  1. Are there missing values or duplicates?
  2. Are values and formats consistent?
  3. What patterns or differences do I see?
  4. What evidence supports my conclusion?
